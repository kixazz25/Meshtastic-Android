⛔⛔ THE FINDING THAT MATTERS: rider-placed points never become graph nodes.

A waypoint tapped on a trail is used to centre the corridor box and then discarded. The ride's origin is whatever pre-existing junction node happens to be nearest — measured today at 600 feet from Fred's trailhead, with no trail in between. Pins behave the same way: ten pins collapsed onto four nodes, one moved 6,001 feet.

⭐ Fred's framing, and it is the correct one: a trailhead sits on a road with spurs radiating from it like a hub. Picking one arbitrary node picks one spoke — and every one of the 2,500 restarts then starts down that spoke. The other directions are not under-explored, they are structurally excluded.

This is not a pin-feature bug. It is the correctness of the whole engine. "Find me a ride in this area" has always meant "find me a ride from one arbitrarily chosen spoke."

⚠⚠ AND IT WAS OBSERVED WEEKS AGO AND EXPLAINED AWAY. Fred raised that rides radiated in one direction. The answer given was that POI distribution dictated it. That explanation was plausible, unfalsifiable without instrumentation, and wrong — it stopped the investigation. The measurement that found the real cause took twenty minutes.

The rule this earns: when the person who uses the app says something looks wrong, the answer is a measurement, not an explanation.

1. 🔴 TOMORROW — the fix, in order

Step 1 — prototype the edge split in Python

Against D:\nav_test\spatial_after.db at Panguitch. No Kotlin until this is proven. The empirical-first rule; today cost a day to changes reasoned about rather than measured.

1. Find the nearest EDGE, not the nearest node — perpendicular
   distance from the point to each line segment in the corridor.
2. Project the point onto that edge (clamped to the segment).
3. Split: A→B becomes A→P and P→B, miles divided at the projection.
4. Insert P into nodes with the RIDER'S coordinates.
5. Rewire adj: P linked to A and B.
6. Add P to mainComponent if A or B is in it.

Step 2 — apply it to the trailhead first

The origin matters more than the pins. With the waypoint as a real node, all spokes are adjacent to the origin and the first hop can go any direction.

⭐ THE SUCCESS TEST, in Fred's words: "i would be happy to see some radiation of direction. more than one spoke ridden."

Four routes fanning out from the hub instead of stacking down one spoke. Visible on the map in one run — no instrumentation needed.

Step 3 — then the pins

Same operation at each pin. Ten pins become ten destinations where they were dropped.

⚠ Watch for, once the origin is fixed: PENALTY = 2.2 is applied to route 1's edges before route 2 is built. If the hub's own road is on route 1, penalising it pushes route 2 away from the hub rather than down a different spoke. May be fine at 2.2, may not — a tuning question with a visible answer.

Step 4 — cap E's insertion

assess() enforces SNAP_POINT_MI = 0.5 and refuses distant pins. E's insertion has no cap at all and silently accepts them — which is how a 6,001 ft pin became a POI. Two snap policies for the same pin; they must agree.

4. ⭐ THE ASYMMETRY THAT HID THE PIN BUG ALL DAY

Two RouteExplorer.Request(...) call sites exist. assess() takes the points as a separate argument; explore() built a Request with no includePoints at all.

So the mileage readout worked perfectly — through the other door — while the routes had no reason to go near the rider's places. The half that measures was wired; the half that builds was not. Every symptom pointed at scoring because the measuring half looked healthy.
