The five steps

  • 1 — Welcome, and say what happens at the end. ⭐ The panel now opens by telling the rider the SHAPE of the whole thing: up to six rides, seen together, compared side by side, keep one and the rest are cleared. A rider who knows a comparison is coming reads every step differently — dropping a pin is contributing to a shortlist rather than committing to a route.
    ⚠ And it sets an expectation the evaluation panel must then meet: six on the map in distinct colours, narrative per ride, promote one and delete the rest. That panel is phase 3 and does not exist yet.
  • 1b — Get on the ground. ⭐ Three ways in: a place by name (town, region, feature), one of your tracks, or one of your waypoints. Then the instruction that matters — zoom to where trails meet a road. That is where a ride can start, and it is a thing a rider can see on satellite imagery without knowing the area.
  • 2 — How long a day? ⭐ NEW. Two sliders, footnoted for what they actually mean: the shortest ride worth taking and the furthest you will go. Together they produce a time window — shortest ÷ fastest pace to furthest ÷ slowest pace — and the rider adjusts until that window is the day they have. Time is the constraint, miles is the control. The 12–18 mph band is measured from real rides and already includes stops, so it is the whole day out, not moving time. It is shown but discouraged: leave it alone until you have ridden enough to know your pace differs.
  • 1 — Find your area. Search a place, or open one of your tracks to put you there. No button: moving the map is the step.
  • 3 — Tap where you will park. ⚠ Not "trailhead" — a staging area is the parking lot, obvious from imagery and needing no trail knowledge. The 600-yard candidate radius is what makes that work.
    ⭐ And the guidance is visual and checkable: pick somewhere several trails leave in different directions, not a dead-end spur. Measured 08-25 — a staging point on a six-node island could reach two of ten pins and produced one route. If one is there, tap it. If not, press and hold one second, name it, save, then tap it. The ride always starts from a saved waypoint.
  • 4 — Points to cover. ⭐ The copy now opens by saying the rider has already done enough — "we have enough now to build you a set of rides" — so pins read as an optional refinement rather than another hurdle. CONTINUE means both "none" and "finished".
    ⚠ FIVE, not ten. PIN_MAX went 10 → 5 on 08-26: ten pins took the tested ceiling from 4 to 11 and the build ran five minutes without finishing. Ten becomes possible again only if the over-five path is built — pins as a fixed skeleton, enriched rather than enumerated.
    ⚠ "We move each one to the nearest trail" is a promise the engine keeps — measured 08-26 at 16–150 ft onto named trails. But "build the rides around them" is intent, not a guarantee: pins are high-value POIs and the search strongly favours them, yet nothing requires them. A test run took two of three. Either the copy stays soft or the engine gains a membership rule.
  • 5 — Finish point. We return to your trailhead unless you drop a pin somewhere else. The loop question answers itself.
  • 6 — Searching.

Worth deciding

  • Pins draw where you tapped, not where they snap. The engine snaps to the nearest routable node within half a mile — so a pin dropped off-trail is accepted but the route may pass a quarter-mile away. Drawing the marker at the snapped position would make that honest.
  • Where MAP TAPS ON/OFF lives — a rider mid-flow needs it to read a trail name.
  • Whether removing a pin renumbers the rest, or leaves a gap.
