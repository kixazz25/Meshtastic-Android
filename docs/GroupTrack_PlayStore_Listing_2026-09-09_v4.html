⛔ POLICY — checked 09-09, and it kills one earlier idea

Play's metadata policy prohibits, and violation can get the app rejected:

  • ⛔ Price language — "free", "no ads", "10% off", "free for a limited time". So "free during early access" CANNOT go in the listing. That was in v1–v3 and it is now out.
  • ⛔ Performance or ranking claims — "best", "#1", "top", "App of the Year", award icons.
  • ⛔ Calls to action — "download now".
  • ⛔ Emojis and ALL CAPS, unless the caps are your brand.

⚠ THIS APPLIES TO SCREENSHOT CAPTIONS TOO, not just the description. Every caption below has been checked.

⭐ What IS allowed: factual capability claims. "2,400 miles that no public source had" is a statement about what the app did, not a ranking claim — it stays.

⚠ LIKELY BLOCKER — the feature graphic

Every listing requires a feature graphic: exactly 1024 × 500 px, JPEG or 24-bit PNG, no transparency. It is the banner at the top of the listing.

Do you have one? If not this blocks publishing, and it is a design job rather than a screenshot. ⭐ Simplest version that would work: the routes-compared map as background, darkened, with the app name and one line of type.

App title — 30 characters

Full description — 4,000 characters

Draft below is ~2,300. ⭐ The overview is lifted from the Quick Start, so the store listing and the first screen a rider sees say the same thing.

An off-road trail app for side-by-side, UTV, ATV, dirt bike and Jeep riders.

It holds the trails and the maps on your device, so it keeps working where there is no signal — which is most of where you ride.

Everything works on its own, for one rider: plan a ride, follow it, record where you went. Add a mesh radio when you want to ride as a group, and every rider appears on your map in real time.

Tell it the ride you want. Get rides worth taking.

Not a route you draw. Not somebody else's GPX file.

Say where you are starting, how far you want to go and how fast you ride. GroupTrack searches your trail network and comes back with several complete options — 74 miles, 77 miles, 68 miles — laid out side by side so you can compare them and pick one.

Each ride arrives described: distance, riding time, and every summit, spring and landmark you will pass, in the order you will reach them. Mile 8, Mountain Spring Peak. Mile 25, Pink Knolls. Mile 30, Red Hill.

It identifies new trails from your ride tracks, then plans routes on them

Every app lets you import your old tracks. They sit on the map as lines you rode once.

GroupTrack does something else with them. Where a ride of yours crosses ground no published survey covers, it becomes a trail — part of your network, searchable, and available to the route planner as a piece it can build with.

So a connector you found three years ago stops being a line in your history and starts turning up in rides you never took, joined to trails you have not ridden yet.

One rider's collection of 88 tracks produced over 1,800 new trails covering 2,400 miles that no public source had. Those rides can only be found here, because no other app knows the trail is there.

Import everything you have — onX, Gaia, anywhere. The more of your riding it knows, the better the rides it can find.

Your whole group on one map, with no cell service

Pair a Meshtastic radio and every rider appears on your map in real time. No towers, no signal, no network subscription.

The lead knows the sweep is still coming. The sweep knows nobody has been left at a junction. Group span, rider count and who is at the tail, on screen, all day.

Everything works offline

Download your state's trail data and satellite or topographic imagery before you leave. Plan routes, follow them, record your ride and track your group with the phone in airplane mode.

Built to be learned in an afternoon

A Quick Start opens the first time you run it: 32 short exercises, each one a tap away from the manual section that explains it. Work through them at the kitchen table and you will know the app before you need it on the trail.

Honest about what it knows

GroupTrack knows a trail is there. It does not know how wide it is, what the surface is like, or what is on it. Check the satellite view before you commit, and check access and ownership the way you would anywhere else.

First run

Setting up a state downloads its full trail dataset — around 30 minutes and a lot of data. Do it on wifi, before the trip, not in the trailhead parking lot.

When the app loads your location, you are ready to play.

⭐ A/B test — Ride Planning vs Group & Mesh

Play Console has free built-in store listing experiments. Test one element at a time and run at least 7 days. Metric: retained first-time installers — installs that kept the app a day or more — not raw installs. A listing can win on installs and lose on retention, and retention is what you actually want.

The question

Which problem brings a rider to the store? "I want better rides" or "I keep losing people on the trail". They are different buyers and the answer decides how you write everything afterwards, including the Facebook posts.

⚠ What NOT to test

  • The title — cannot be tested, needs a full app update.
  • Two things at once. If you change the short description and the screenshots together you learn nothing about either.
  • Anything, before you have traffic. An experiment needs enough installs to reach significance. With a handful of early-access testers it will never conclude — ⭐ so run the A/B once the Facebook posts are driving real volume, not on day one.

⛔ The group shot has to be retaken

The one in the collection is suburban New Hampshire — an insurance office, a banquet centre, Griffin Park — and reads as a road map. It also shows 0.0 mi span, 1 cart, which is a demo rather than a convoy.

Retaking it in New Hampshire is fine, with three conditions:

  • A dirt or forest road, not a residential street. Nobody will identify the state; they will notice a driveway.
  • Real span on the HUD. Park the radios a mile or two apart. The numbers are the point.
  • ⭐ TOPO+, not satellite. Topo hides the parking lots and cul-de-sacs that gave the last one away.

Every other shot is real Utah trail data, so the listing as a whole reads correctly.

Category and tags

  • Maps & Navigation. Not Sports — that is fitness trackers, and your buyer is not browsing there. Not Travel & Local; that is restaurants.
  • Tags (up to five, in Store settings): Navigation, Maps, Outdoors, Off-road, Trip Planning.
  • ⭐ Tags drive the "similar apps" row. A rider looking at onX Offroad or Gaia GPS is your best prospect — they already know they need this kind of app.

Still to decide

  • Feature graphic — 1024 × 500. Blocker if absent.
  • App title — decide before publishing; not A/B testable.
  • Does the radio get named? Tracking needs a Meshtastic radio and the automated setup is T1000-E only. Saying so costs some installs and prevents the one-star that opens "I could not get tracking to work."
  • Coverage. Trail data is per-state and the app is built around Utah. A rider in Michigan finding thin data is a bad review. ⚠ Consider naming which states have good coverage.
  • ⛔ Price messaging has nowhere to go in the listing. If early access pricing matters, it belongs in the Facebook posts, not the store page.

Acquisition

The Facebook groups are the whole channel

Eight to ten Utah UTV groups is worth more than anything else available, and they punish anything that reads as an ad.

⭐ Post a result, not a product. A screenshot of a route through ground people know, app incidental — "found this loop off Jockey Road, 77 miles, eight summits." Let somebody ask what it is. The routes-compared screen does this on its own.

⚠ Price and early-access terms CAN be said here. The store listing is where they cannot.

The claim nobody can match

"2,400 miles of Utah trail that onX does not show." True, checkable, and the only line a competitor cannot copy.

Jamborees

Rattlesnake, Red Rim, Yankee Meadows and Broken Ridge all appear in your own track names. A jamboree is a hundred riders with the same problem in one parking lot.

⚠ Fix this first

The 30-minute first download is the biggest install-to-uninstall risk in the product. It is in the description, and it belongs in the first line of any Facebook post — a rider who starts it on mobile data at a trailhead is a lost user and a one-star.
