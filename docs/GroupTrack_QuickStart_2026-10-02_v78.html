1 · Install, and first start

GroupTrack keeps your trails, tracks, routes and downloaded maps in its own storage on the device. Nothing you do here puts files anywhere else, and you are not asked for access to your phone's folders.

If you are updating from an older version, the first launch moves your existing data across for you — follow the prompt and leave the screen open while it works.

Grant the app the authorities it needs — location, all the time. Follow the prompts.

Fill in your rider profile — your callsign and email. GroupTrack asks once, before the map opens.

Answer the radio question — Do you have a mesh radio to use with GroupTrack? YES: switch the radio on; GRP Awareness opens — connect it, then Select a ride and apply settings to the radio → GroupTrack default. Pairing code 123456 unless your radio shows another. NO: do it later in GRP Awareness, before your first ride.

Choose your home state and let it download the trails, so they work with no signal.

When the app loads your location, you are ready to play.

That is the whole of it. The detail below is worth reading when you have a moment — none of it is needed to get going.

Loading the trails for your home state

On first launch GroupTrack asks for location, then asks which state you ride in and offers the one it thinks you are in.

Pick your home state and load the trail data.

Pick your state and let it run. It takes about twenty minutes and pulls a lot of data, so do it on wifi. It is not something to start at the trailhead.

What it is fetching, and why it takes that long: GroupTrack does not use one trail map. It pulls from every public source that covers your state and merges them, so a trail one agency published and another did not still appears. For Utah that is seven catalogues plus the OpenStreetMap extract:

  • Utah Trails and Pathways — the state's own trail record
  • US Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, USGS National Trails — the federal agencies that manage the ground
  • Arizona State Parks — border coverage
  • OpenStreetMap — the largest single source, and the one that carries tracks and unclassified roads the agencies do not list

Another state draws on a different set. The app picks the sources that cover the area you asked for.

It also downloads the land ownership record for your state, which is what lets it tell public ground from private.

When it finishes you get a summary listing every source and what came in. It waits for you — if you put the tablet down and come back later, it is still there. ⚠ If a line says a source failed, the rest still loaded; you can reload the state later from the same place.
Choosing what appears on the map

GroupTrack holds around 146,000 trails for Utah. Most of what is in that number is ground you will never ride — footpaths, residential streets, cycle paths through town. Choosing what appears is how the map becomes readable.

Map Features › Trails › SELECT — choose which kinds of trail appear.

What you get out of the box

GroupTrack starts by showing motorized trails on public land only. Hiking, biking and equestrian trails are hidden, and so is private ground.

⚠ If a trail you know exists is not on the map, that is why — not a fault. It is a deliberate choice for an off-road app, and it is the first thing worth knowing.

Changing it

Map Features › Trails › SELECT. The same button you use to choose which tracks, waypoints or routes appear.

  • Include private land — one tick. Near a town this is what removes the residential street grid, which is usually why a map is hard to read there.
  • The trail types, in two columns: motorized and non-motorized. Tap a row to hide that type; tap a column heading for the whole group.

And OFF on the Trails row hides every trail in one tap, for when you want to see the ground.

Not sure what a category is?

Press CATEGORIES EXPLAINED at the foot of the Map Keys panel. It says what each trail type actually means — which are posted for machines, which are open unless a sign says otherwise, and which came from your own rides.

The colours are yours

Whatever you set is what the map draws, every time you open it. You do not need to visit the key panel again for your choices to take effect.

Solid lines are motorized. Dotted lines are not. That is the distinction worth learning, and it is one you can read in sunlight without picking colours apart.

Tap Map Keys to see what each line means, and tap any name in that list to change its colour, pattern or thickness. It shows the line as it is beside the line as it would be, so you can compare before committing. What you choose is saved.

Your rider profile

Your profile — your callsign and your email — is who you are in GroupTrack. Your callsign is your name on every map and in every report your radio sends; your email is how rides reach you. It is private: it stays on your tablet, and goes only to the riders you ride with. GroupTrack asks for it the first time it opens; change it any time in Settings → Your Profile.

Opening your first ride

The first time you tap a ride someone sent you, Android asks which app should open it: choose GroupTrack, then Always. The route, trailhead and waypoints arrive, and the maps along the route start downloading. If ride files open somewhere else: Android Settings → Apps → GroupTrack → Open by default → Clear defaults, then tap the file again.

★ Create your first ride — new in 2.7a

Riding together starts with a ride. GroupTrack's community begins with the ride package — a route, starting at its defined trailhead, on a set date and time, with the maps it needs and its radio configuration. Send it to friends and riders who want to ride with you.

One click, and everyone has the same preparation: the trailhead marked on their map, the route outlined to follow, the required maps downloaded along it. At the trailhead, check-in applies the ride's radio configuration for them. The fastest way to create one is a map touch: tap a route or a track on the Ride Planning Map, then CREATE RIDE — Manual: Map touches, the fastest way to Create a Ride.

No more night before the ride spent downloading an area and hoping the trail goes where you think it does. No one asks where to meet, and no one waits at the trailhead while someone else catches up. Everyone's time goes to riding.

Create a ride and send it
  1. On the Ride Planning Map, tap a route. A route the planner built opens its description — tap CREATE RIDE; a route you drew opens its details — tap ADD A RIDE. (From a track you recorded: tap it, then CREATE RIDE.)
  2. The ride form opens with the route, its trailhead and its picture. Set the date and time, fill in the rest, SAVE RIDE.
  3. Work with Rides → Rides, tap your ride, Share — the invite goes to your riding partners.

2 · Exercises — the Ride Planning Map

What a ride is

A ride is everything a group needs to ride together, in one package: the route and its trailhead, waypoints, the maps for riding off grid, and the radio settings for the ride's network. The organizer builds it on a route — or a track they rode — and sends it as a Ride invite. Each rider — any GroupTrack user — taps it: GroupTrack creates the route, its trailhead and the ride's description on the tablet, and imports the maps needed to ride it off grid. Then they ride; with a radio, riders also check in at the trailhead.

4 · Group awareness

Add a radio to experience full group awareness while riding — no new software, nothing to learn. Your community comes onto the trail with you. Group awareness is seeing your group on the map with no cell service — every cart where it is, with its callsign and role. It needs a Meshtastic radio for each rider; GroupTrack works with any Meshtastic radio and sets it up for you. Checking in to a ride puts your radio on the ride's network and announces your role — the check-in is part of group awareness: riding solo, you never see its screens. Riding alone? Skip this section.
